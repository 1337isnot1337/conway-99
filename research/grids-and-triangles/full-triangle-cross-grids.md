# Grids at different points of a full triangle

Let $T$ be a triangle and index the grids on it by $i$. At each vertex $x$ of $T$, such a grid contributes one further axis, consisting of $x$ and two points of its six-point prism. If the two axes from components $i,j$ span another grid through $x$, call it $G_x(ij)$. Its four points away from these axes lie in the set $W$ of sixty vertices with no neighbor in $T$. For example, each such point already sees two near points of $x$, so it sees neither other point of $T$.

**For distinct vertices $x,y$ of $T$, any two grids $G_x(ij)$ and $G_y(kl)$ are disjoint.** This conclusion does not require $T$ to be full. Where the index pairs intersect, a common point in $W$ would have two different neighbors in the same grid on $T$, contradicting its perfect domination. Suppose the indices are disjoint and there is a common point $w$. The intersection can only be $w$: two nonadjacent common points determine a unique grid, and two adjacent common points would force their entire triangle into the intersection, which lies in a four-point square.

In each grid the four points nonadjacent to $w$ form a square. A point of either square already has both common neighbors of $w$ inside its own grid. Thus it has no neighbor among the neighbors of $w$ in the other grid. Perfect domination gives a matching between the two opposite squares. The square on the $x$ side contains the two prism sites on components $i,j$ opposite the signs seen by $w$. Neither of those sites sees $y$ or either of the two prism sites of the opposite square on components $k,l$. Distinct prisms on $T$ are anticomplete. This leaves just one point of the opposite square for the two sites, contrary to the matching.

## Three ten-grid roots

Now suppose $T$ lies on six grids and all three of its vertices lie on ten grids. Index the prisms by $0,\ldots,5$. At each vertex $x$ the [ten-grid classification](ten-grid-bound.md#four-patterns-at-the-upper-bound) gives four more grid pairs on these indices: a four-cycle or a triangle with a pendant edge, with two isolates in either case. Write $D_x$ for this four-edge set.

Each $w\in W$ sees one point in each of the six prisms and two in each of the three fibers of $T$. Its fiber choices therefore form an ordered partition $(a,b,c)$ of the six indices into three unordered pairs. Let $t_{abc}$ be the number of occurrences. Each pair in a specified position occurs four times, one point for each sign choice in its fiber. Two fixed, distinct indices in two fixed, distinct positions occur together eight times: the two signed sites on the first prism and the two on the second give four pairs of nonadjacent sites with no common core neighbor, and each pair has two common neighbors in $W$.

If $a\in D_x$, the four rows with first pair $a$ are the square in $G_x(a)$. For each index $h\notin a$ the four sites in prism $h$ in the other two fibers each have a unique neighbor in this square. Hence $h$ appears exactly twice in each of the other two positions. On the four indices outside $a$, the second pairs form a two-regular multigraph with four edges. Complementary pairs have equal multiplicities: adding the degrees of the first two indices and comparing with the other two proves the equality. By disjointness a table entry must vanish if two of its pairs are in the respective $D$'s. Thus at least one partition of the four remaining indices into two pairs has both pairs absent from both other $D$'s.

The [integer table enumeration](../../experiments/workspace/full_triangle_ten_roots.py) permutes the six indices and the three points of $T$. The last split condition leaves 116 triples. Exactly 17 admit a nonnegative integer table satisfying the counts, and there are 1,899 tables for their chosen representatives. In every one, **at least two different index pairs occur in all three graphs $D_x,D_y,D_z$**.

Run `make verify-full-triangle-ten-roots` for the table enumeration.
